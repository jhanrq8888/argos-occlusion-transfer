\title{Gains on Synthetic Occlusion Do Not Anticipate\\Robustness to Real Occlusion in Person Detection}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Celia Patricia Apaza Hilasaca, Brayan Raul Condori Quispe, Jhan Logan Ramos Quispe}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Universidad Peruana Uni\'on (UPeU), Juliaca, Peru}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Synthetic occlusion augmentation is used to make person detectors robust to partial visibility, yet its benefit is usually measured on the same artificial occlusion used for training. We ask whether it transfers to real, human-annotated occlusion. We compare a baseline and an augmented model, with the same data and hyperparameters, in 18 blocks that vary the occluder (patches, silhouettes, diffusion, real geometry and depth order), the detector (YOLOv11 at two sizes, YOLO26s, RT-DETR-l, RetinaNet and Faster R-CNN), the training domain and the amount of data (117 runs). Each block has a synthetic control and three real-occlusion sources, by day and by night, and its hypotheses and rules were fixed before training it. A cell passes if AP@0.5 improves by at least 2 points with a 95\,\% interval that excludes zero. With one-stage and transformer detectors, all 13 controls pass and 4 of 39 real cells pass, none both stable across seeds and specific to occluded people; 24 rule out a 2-point gain and, with equal training exposure, the mean effect is null. A single combination transfers: the two-stage Faster R-CNN with silhouettes (NightOwls +3.3 and CityPersons +2.3). It does not with diffusion or depth occluders, nor does RetinaNet, one-stage with the same backbone and recipe, with silhouettes. Within Faster R-CNN, the largest control (+20.4, diffusion) does not transfer and a smaller one (+14.7, silhouettes) does: the synthetic gain does not anticipate the real one and is not enough to claim robustness to real occlusion.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Person detection supports public safety, surveillance and mobile robotics, mostly with conventional RGB cameras. Occlusion, a major cause of pedestrian-detection failures~\cite{Dollar2012}, and low illumination~\cite{Loh2019} often coincide at night. In our data, a YOLOv11s fine-tuned on ExDark drops from 0.82 AP@0.5 on unoccluded people to 0.24 on severely occluded ones.

A widespread remedy is \emph{synthetic occlusion augmentation}: during training, parts of the person are erased or covered~\cite{Zhong2020,Shang2018synthocc,Chi2020pedhunter}. Its benefit is usually measured on the same artificial occlusion or within the training set, and rarely on people \emph{really} occluded by a car, a pole or another person. If it does not reach real occlusion, synthetic benchmarks overstate robustness.

\textbf{Gap.} In the literature reviewed (Section~II) we found no study that (i) measures transfer to annotated real occlusion with a synthetic control confirming that the augmentation was learned, (ii) does so on several independent real sources, (iii) tests alternative explanations (occluder, detector, domain, data volume, exposure) with controlled experiments and (iv) fixes hypotheses and rules before seeing results.

\textbf{Objectives and contribution.} The objectives are to measure the degradation caused by occlusion (OE1), the effect of augmentation on synthetic and real occlusion by occluder (OE2), detector (OE3), and domain and data (OE4), and its mechanism (OE5)\footnote{The mapping of objectives, method, evidence and pending work, and all material cited as ``supplement'', are in the supplementary material.}. We contribute (1) controlled evidence in 18 blocks with five kinds of occluder and six detectors (one-stage, two-stage and transformer), including the only case of transfer (Faster R-CNN with silhouettes) and two pre-specified tests that bound it; (2) a single count, with intervals and split by training exposure, showing that the synthetic gain does not anticipate the real one; and (3) a reusable evaluation protocol: synthetic control, several real sources, equal exposure and a rule fixed in advance.

\section{Related Work and Explanatory Frame}
\textbf{Detection under occlusion and low light.} Recent detectors tackle occlusion with architectural changes and report gains within one benchmark: PRNet++ refines anchors from visible parts and evaluates occlusion subsets on CityPersons, Caltech and ECP-night~\cite{Song2022prnet}; MLFFCSP fuses multi-level features with Random Erasing~\cite{Huan2023mlffcsp}. For night driving, a YOLOX with domain transfer (degraded daytime plus night data) raises mAP by 5.9 points without stratifying by occlusion~\cite{Yi2022yolox}; POP-YOLOv8~\cite{Liu2026pop} and BNE-DETR~\cite{Li2026bne} report aggregate metrics.

\textbf{Augmentation as a training signal and as a test.} Random Erasing~\cite{Zhong2020} inspired pedestrian variants evaluated within CityPersons or CrowdHuman~\cite{Shang2018synthocc,Chi2020pedhunter}, and Copy-Paste pastes cut-out objects without modelling context~\cite{Ghiasi2021copypaste}, a direct antecedent of our depth-ordered occluders. Three works frame the contrast. Kassaw et al.\ find that synthetic occlusion \emph{ranks} already-trained classifiers much like real occlusion~\cite{Kassaw2026occl}: it is useful to compare models. Taori et al.\ show that robustness gained against synthetic shifts barely carries over to natural shifts, except with larger and more diverse data~\cite{Taori2020shifts}, and Pytel et al.\ that, in pose estimation, occlusion augmentation does not solve occlusion~\cite{Pytel2021tilting}. We ask whether the gain an occlusion augmentation produces in detection, measured on synthetic occlusion, anticipates its gain on real occlusion.

\textbf{Structural alternatives and explanatory frame.} Compositional networks represent objects by parts and are more robust to partial occlusion than data augmentation~\cite{Kortylewski2020compnet,Wang2020compnet}; WALT learns amodal representations from real occlusion in fixed cameras~\cite{Reddy2022walt}, and domain adaptation attacks the night gap from the data side~\cite{Yi2022yolox,Song2022prnet}. Our study does not evaluate those routes: it is limited to pixel-level augmentation of RGB images for modal detection. As an explanatory frame we use shortcut learning~\cite{Geirhos2020shortcut}: a network can lower its loss by learning the features of the artificial occluder without learning to detect incomplete people, and pedestrian detectors already generalise poorly across datasets~\cite{Hasan2021elephant,Sukesh2024camped}. The review started from three Scopus searches exported on 13 Oct 2026 (155, 8 and 71 records; strings in the supplement), which yielded the five seed articles~\cite{Song2022prnet,Reddy2022walt,Huan2023mlffcsp,Sukesh2024camped,Yi2022yolox}, and was completed with targeted searches in Crossref and arXiv.

\section{Method}
\textbf{Design, hypotheses and rule.} This is a controlled experiment: in each block, the baseline (V1) and the augmented model (V3) share data, hyperparameters, base augmentation and machine; V3 adds occlusion augmentation in training only. All transfer hypotheses predict that the deficit persists (no transfer). The prediction is \emph{confirmed} if fewer than two of the three real sources \emph{pass}, i.e.\ improve AP@0.5 by at least +2 percentage points (pp, mean over seeds) with a 95\,\% image CI that excludes zero, while the synthetic control does pass; if the control fails, the block is inconclusive. H11 predicted the opposite (a gain on the most occluded people), H16a and H16b that the Faster R-CNN transfer would extend to other occluders, and H13 an order of realism. The original design also included an attention module (variants V2 and V4; H1, H2 and H5), with no gain on real occlusion (supplement). Each hypothesis, its rule and its code were fixed with a SHA-256 cryptographic fingerprint before training its block and none was reworded; the fingerprint proves that nothing changed since the date recorded by the project, but there was no external registry. The table of hypotheses, fingerprints, dates and first runs is in the supplement. The question was reoriented after the first experiment and H9b--H17 were designed after seeing earlier results and reviews: the programme is adaptive.

\textbf{Data.} ExDark~\cite{Loh2019}, with the split of Cui et al.~\cite{Cui2021}, has 2,658 images with people and 7,460 instances; 1,183 of those images are used for training. Real-occlusion sources: (1) NightOwls~\cite{Neumann2019}, at night, with its authors' occlusion attribute (530 occluded validation pedestrians; up to 20,000 training frames); (2) CityPersons~\cite{Zhang2017}, by day, official \emph{heavy} split (visibility 20--65\,\%, 733 instances); (3) an ExDark proxy that estimates occlusion between people from box overlap (101 severe instances), weakly correlated with annotated visibility (Spearman 0.24). Manual visual grading was discarded as ground truth ($\kappa$ 0.06--0.60). The three datasets are used under their non-commercial research licences and their images are not redistributed.

\textbf{Augmentation and control.} Occluder families: opaque or textured patches; masked silhouettes of real objects and people; occluders painted by Stable Diffusion 1.5 with ten COCO objects~\cite{Rombach2022ldm}; silhouettes placed with real CityPersons visibility patterns; and object cut-outs placed in front of the person by the monocular depth of Depth Anything V2~\cite{Yang2024depthv2}. V3 occludes 1--3 people per image with coverage 0.30--0.85. Each family's \emph{control} applies the same generator to every person in ExDark test with coverage 0.70--1.00, using images and occluders not seen in training (disjoint cut-out banks or new generations). It measures whether the augmentation was learned; to check whether it measures more than the occluder itself, we evaluate each family on the controls of the others (cross-family matrix).

\textbf{Detectors and exposure.} YOLOv11s, YOLOv11m~\cite{Ultralytics2024}, YOLO26s~\cite{Ultralytics2026yolo26} (SGD, lr 0.002, batch 16), RT-DETR-l~\cite{Zhao2024rtdetr} (AdamW, $10^{-4}$) and, from torchvision with the same ResNet-50 FPN backbone, Faster R-CNN~\cite{Ren2017faster} and RetinaNet~\cite{Lin2017focal} (SGD, 0.01 and 0.005, batch 8), COCO-pretrained~\cite{Lin2014}, 640-px input, Ultralytics 8.4.143 (full recipe in the supplement). In the first blocks each variant was selected at its best validation epoch (patience 20, up to 100 epochs), which left \emph{unequal} exposure: in H4, H4-Y26, H6 and H8, V1 was selected at epochs 1--51 and V3 at 31--89. From H9b on, V1 and V3 train a fixed number of epochs and are evaluated with the last weights; those blocks are the primary evidence. Machines: a laptop with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5050 Laptop GPU (8 GB) and, for H11, H12 and H14--H17, a rented RTX 4090.

\textbf{Evaluation.} AP@0.5 of class \emph{person} per occlusion stratum, with instances of other strata as \emph{ignore} regions, as in CityPersons~\cite{Zhang2017}. Percentile bootstrap CIs over images (1,000 replicates)~\cite{Efron1993} and, as sensitivity, hierarchical over seeds and images (2,000). The +2 pp threshold is the smallest effect of interest: it exceeds the between-seed SD of V1 AP on the real sources (median 1.5 pp). A cell \emph{rules out} a relevant gain if the upper limit of its CI is below +2 (one-sided non-superiority test~\cite{Lakens2017tost}). Miss rate MR$^{-2}$~\cite{Dollar2012} and AP@0.5:0.95 are secondary (supplement).

\section{Results}
\textbf{Count.} All figures are V3$-$V1 in AP@0.5 (Table~\ref{tab:res}). In the 13 comparable blocks with one-stage and transformer detectors (all but H7, which has no control, and the four torchvision blocks, added after the count), all 13 controls pass, from +2.6 to +60.9 pp. Of the 39 real cells, 4 pass: the ExDark proxy in H9 (+2.82) and H14 (+5.49), which do not survive the hierarchical interval, and CityPersons in H10 (+2.37) and its silhouette arm (+2.26), where unoccluded people gain more (+5.3). Another 24 cells rule out a +2 pp gain (19 with the hierarchical interval). Without the ExDark proxy, 2 of 26 cells pass and no block reaches two sources. With H15--H17 there are 17 passing controls and 6 of 51 real cells pass, 29 of which rule out +2 pp.

\textbf{Exposure explains the harm, not the absence of gain.} With equal exposure (21 cells, H9b to H14) the mean effect is $+0.02$ pp, 3 cells pass and 3 are negative with CI (with the 12 of H15--H17, $+0.01$ over 33); with unequal exposure (12 cells of H4, H4-Y26, H6 and H8) the mean effect is $-4.94$ pp and 6 are negative with CI. The large harm of silhouettes on NightOwls ($-16.8$ in H4) shrinks to $-1.12$ with fixed epochs (H10-s): it reflects domain overfitting induced by the selection rule. What holds in both cases is the absence of gain.

\textbf{Detector, domain and data.} The pattern repeats with RT-DETR-l (H8), YOLO26s and YOLOv11m (H12), which learns the augmentation more strongly than YOLOv11s (controls +14.45 and +19.04) and transfers it to no source. With Faster R-CNN (H15), in contrast, the control rises +14.72 and NightOwls (+3.28 [+1.64; +4.79]) and CityPersons (+2.33 [+1.01; +3.69]) pass, both also with the hierarchical interval: H15 is refuted. On NightOwls unoccluded people improve more (+4.37) than occluded ones, but on CityPersons occluded people gain more (\emph{heavy} +2.33, \emph{partial} +2.57) than visible ones (\emph{bare} +0.88); on ExDark nothing changes, and the miss rate drops by less than 2 pp. The two pre-specified tests that bound it fail: with diffusion and depth occluders (H16a and H16b), Faster R-CNN learns the augmentation (+20.35 and +9.63) and does not transfer it (0 of 3, no real cell above +0.92), and RetinaNet with silhouettes (H17), which differs only in being one-stage, does not either (0 of 3; it drops in every real stratum, more for visible people). A Faster R-CNN V1 retrained on another machine changed by $-1.92$ pp on CityPersons and +3.42 on ExDark: the variation between machines is of the order of the H15 gains. Retraining on CityPersons (H6) removes the negative sign, but the control gains +23.74 and \emph{heavy} +1.82; with fixed epochs and new seeds, the gain on the most occluded people (visibility 0.20--0.50) is +1.44 [$-$0.64; +3.34]: H11 is not confirmed. With 20,000 NightOwls images (H7), $-1.43$ [$-$3.98; +1.00].

\textbf{Occluder, geometry and depth.} Neither diffusion occluders (H9, H9b), nor real geometry (H10; against plain silhouettes $-0.93$, +0.10 and $-1.38$ pp), nor objects placed in front of the person by depth (H14) meet the rule. With five seeds, CityPersons \emph{heavy} with silhouettes survives the hierarchical interval (+2.06 [+0.26; +4.17]), but unoccluded people improve twice as much. In the cross-family matrix, each family also gains on the controls of the others: 13 of 20 cross cells pass (for example, silhouettes gain +15.8 on the geometry control and depth order +11.7), and only patches stay close to their own control (+0.8 to +3.0 on the rest). The control does not measure memory of specific occluders but a general adaptation to synthetic occlusion, which does not transfer to real occlusion either.

\textbf{Predictiveness and realism (post hoc).} Across blocks there is no reliable association between synthetic and real gain (Fig.~\ref{fig:disp}); in a mixed model with a random intercept per block, one SD of synthetic gain (14 pp) is associated with +0.11 pp of real gain [$-$1.74; +1.95]. With 13 blocks a moderate association on NightOwls cannot be ruled out. To measure realism (H13), a linear classifier on DINOv2 features~\cite{Oquab2024dinov2} separates 400 really occluded CityPersons pedestrians from those occluded with each family with AUC 0.935--0.993, and from the same people unoccluded with 0.876; CLIP~\cite{Radford2021clip} agrees. The kernel inception distance~\cite{Binkowski2018kid}, which compares distributions, puts silhouettes, geometry and diffusion slightly closer to real occlusion than unoccluded people (unnormalised DINOv2 features, 400 per set: 1.57, 1.42 and 1.33 versus 1.75), with overlapping intervals; measured on the occluded region only, no family comes close to real occluders (KID 2.5--3.4, versus 3.6 without an occluder and 0.01 between two halves of real occluders). What did help heavily occluded people was annotated in-domain data: training on CityPersons raises its \emph{heavy} subset to 0.59, against at most 0.24 without training there; OWLv2~\cite{Minderer2023owlv2} and Grounding DINO~\cite{Liu2024gdino} without fine-tuning do not match it.

\textbf{Mechanism (exploratory).} With augmentation, recall on occluded NightOwls pedestrians falls in every block with diagnostics, and with silhouettes and diffusion false positives fall too: the models detect fewer occluded people, more confidently.

\section{Discussion}
The results agree with Taori et al.~\cite{Taori2020shifts} and Pytel et al.~\cite{Pytel2021tilting}: robustness gained against a synthetic perturbation does not carry over to the real one, and what helped most was real in-domain data. They do not contradict Kassaw et al.~\cite{Kassaw2026occl}: synthetic occlusion can rank already-trained models and still fail to anticipate how much an augmentation trained with it helps. They are consistent with a shortcut~\cite{Geirhos2020shortcut}: the model learns the artificial occluder, which remains distinguishable from real occlusion (H13), but that mechanism has not been tested confirmatorily. Faster R-CNN with silhouettes is the only exception: with a control almost equal to that of YOLOv11m (+14.72 versus +14.45) it transfers on two sources, and on CityPersons in an occlusion-specific way. It is not an effect of the two stages or of the silhouettes alone, because it appears neither with other occluders (H16) nor with RetinaNet (H17): it depends on the combination, and its size is of the order of the variation between machines. What the synthetic control does not allow is to know in advance which combination will transfer: within the same detector, the highest control does not transfer. The conclusion is limited to pixel-level RGB augmentation; it does not refute compositional architectures~\cite{Kortylewski2020compnet,Wang2020compnet}, amodal supervision~\cite{Reddy2022walt} or domain adaptation~\cite{Yi2022yolox}.

\textbf{Limitations.} \emph{Architecture:} six detectors, with YOLOv11 at two sizes; a single two-stage detector, with one backbone and one training set, and runs of one V1 on two machines differ by up to 3.4 pp. \emph{Data:} CityPersons is daytime and NightOwls uses a vehicle camera, so no source reproduces fixed surveillance in the dark; the ExDark proxy captures only occlusion between people and two of the four passing cells come from it; everything is AP@0.5 of one class. \emph{Evaluation protocol:} three seeds per cell (five in the extension); the 39 cells share baselines and test sets and are not independent tests, with no correction for multiplicity; the first blocks have unequal exposure; the fingerprint has no third-party timestamp, and realism was measured in feature spaces, not perceptually.

\textbf{Future directions.} (1) Occlusion mined from real images and amodal supervision~\cite{Reddy2022walt}. (2) Domain adaptation for night~\cite{Yi2022yolox} and part-based architectures~\cite{Wang2020compnet}. (3) Repeating the protocol on real night surveillance video annotated by visibility, with tracking: ByteTrack~\cite{Zhang2022bytetrack} and BoT-SORT~\cite{Aharon2022botsort} keep identities through brief occlusion by recovering low-confidence boxes, but only if the detector emits them, so our result concerns the detector that feeds them. (4) Measuring speed on an embedded device. For practice: a synthetic occlusion benchmark is a control that an augmentation works, but claiming robustness requires a set, even a small one, of annotated real occlusion from the deployment domain.

\section{Conclusion}
In 18 blocks with five kinds of occluder, six detectors, three training domains and up to five seeds, synthetic occlusion augmentation always improved detection on synthetic occlusion. On real occlusion, with equal exposure its mean effect was null, and only one combination, Faster R-CNN with silhouettes, transferred on two sources; the same detector with other occluders and RetinaNet with silhouettes did not. The synthetic gain did not anticipate which combination would transfer. Annotated in-domain data helped occluded people in that domain far more. Occlusion robustness should be evaluated on annotated real occlusion together with a synthetic control.
