\title{Gains on Synthetic Occlusion Are Not Enough: A Controlled Study of the Transfer to Real Occlusion in Person Detection}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Celia Patricia Apaza Hilasaca, Brayan Raul Condori Quispe, Jhan Logan Ramos Quispe}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Universidad Peruana Uni\'on (UPeU), Juliaca, Peru}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Synthetic occlusion augmentation is used to make person detectors robust to partial visibility, yet its benefit is usually measured on the same artificial occlusion. We ask whether that gain is enough to expect one on real occlusion in other datasets. In 21 controlled blocks (133 runs, including those of an initial design with an attention module), a baseline and an augmented model share data and hyperparameters; the blocks vary the occluder, the detector (six; four with equal exposure, one- and two-stage), the domain and the augmentation dose, and are measured on a synthetic control, on two sources of annotated real occlusion (by day and by night) and on an approximate proxy in ExDark, with hypotheses registered internally with a cryptographic fingerprint before training. In the 20 blocks with a control, augmentation improves that control (+2.6 to +60.9 percentage points, pp, of AP@0.5). With the usual dose and equal exposure (12 blocks), a post hoc mixed model estimates between $-1.1$ and +1.0 pp depending on the source, hardly compatible with a mean gain of 2 pp. Only Faster R-CNN with silhouettes improved two real sources; at the usual dose it did not meet the rule again with five seeds, and at a high dose it improved unoccluded people by the same pp. With 1,183 fine-tuning images from COCO, pixel-level augmentation and AP@0.5, passing a synthetic control is not enough to claim robustness to real occlusion.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Person detection with RGB cameras is used in video surveillance and mobile robotics, which also operate at night. In that setting, occlusion, a major cause of pedestrian-detection failures~\cite{Dollar2012}, and low light~\cite{Loh2019} coincide. In our data, the baselines lose 0.39 to 0.62 AP@0.5 on occluded people relative to visible ones, by day and by night (Section~IV).

A widespread remedy is \emph{synthetic occlusion augmentation}: during training, parts of the person are erased or covered~\cite{Zhong2020,Shang2018synthocc,Chi2020pedhunter}. Its benefit is usually measured on the same artificial occlusion or, when real occlusion is used, within the training dataset (CityPersons, CrowdHuman~\cite{Shao2018crowdhuman}); rarely on genuinely occluded people in other datasets. If the synthetic gain does not carry over, synthetic benchmarks overstate the robustness of the surveillance systems validated on them. In Peru, municipal security services had 14,722 operational video-surveillance cameras in 2023~\cite{INEI2024renamu}; with no public Peruvian dataset of people occluded at night that we know of, those systems rely on models trained on foreign data, like those used in this study.

\textbf{Gap.} In the literature reviewed (Section~II) we found no study that (i) measures the effect on annotated real occlusion with a synthetic control confirming that the augmentation was learned, (ii) with several real sources, (iii) tests alternative explanations (occluder, detector, domain, exposure, dose) with controlled experiments and (iv) fixes hypotheses and rules before seeing the results.

\textbf{Question, objectives and contribution.} Is a gain on synthetic occlusion enough to expect a real gain? The general objective is to determine whether synthetic occlusion augmentation that improves a synthetic control also improves the detection of genuinely occluded people. The specific objectives are to measure the baseline's degradation under occlusion (OE1, AP@0.5 by stratum); the effect of augmentation (V3$-$V1) by occluder and dose (OE2), detector (OE3), and training domain (OE4; data volume was only explored); and to explore its mechanism (OE5: specificity, visible box and occluder realism). We contribute (1) controlled evidence in 21 blocks with five kinds of occluder, six detectors (four with equal exposure) and three doses; (2) a global mixed-model estimate and a specificity analysis showing that passing the synthetic control does not guarantee a real gain, and that the only transfer that passes the rule on two sources, at a high dose and with 3 seeds, improves unoccluded people by the same pp; and (3) a minimum protocol (positive control, V1 and V3 on the same machine, fixed epochs, 5 seeds, several sources, specificity and a prior rule), met in full only by H18a.

\section{Related work and explanatory framework}
\textbf{Detection under occlusion and low light.} Pedestrian detectors tackle occlusion through architecture and evaluate within one benchmark: by parts or visible boxes~\cite{Zhang2018orcnn,Zhou2018bibox,Song2022prnet} or by fusing levels with Random Erasing~\cite{Huan2023mlffcsp}; at night, a YOLOX with domain transfer improves without stratifying by occlusion~\cite{Yi2022yolox}.

\textbf{Augmentation as a training signal and as a test.} Random Erasing~\cite{Zhong2020} inspired pedestrian variants evaluated within CityPersons or CrowdHuman~\cite{Shang2018synthocc,Chi2020pedhunter}; Copy-Paste~\cite{Ghiasi2021copypaste} and A-Fast-RCNN~\cite{Wang2017afast} paste objects or occlude features without measuring carry-over to other datasets. Synthetic-corruption benchmarks~\cite{Michaelis2019winter} measure how much a detector withstands without checking whether gains carry over. Robustness to synthetic shifts barely carries over to natural shifts~\cite{Taori2020shifts}; it does in some cases~\cite{Hendrycks2021manyfaces} and depends on the similarity between augmentation and test~\cite{Mintun2021interaction}. Synthetic occlusion \emph{ranks} already-trained classifiers much as real occlusion does~\cite{Kassaw2026occl}, and occlusion augmentation does not solve occlusion in pose estimation~\cite{Pytel2021tilting}. For pedestrians, style-transfer augmentation was evaluated across datasets without stratifying by occlusion~\cite{Cygert2020robust}, and a review places augmentation alongside part-based models and amodal supervision~\cite{Saleh2021occlusion}. Compositional networks represent objects by parts~\cite{Kortylewski2020compnet,Wang2020compnet}; WALT learns amodal representations from real occlusion~\cite{Reddy2022walt}. As a framework we use shortcut learning~\cite{Geirhos2020shortcut}: a network can reduce its loss by learning the artificial occluder without learning to detect incomplete people, and pedestrian detectors already generalize poorly across datasets~\cite{Hasan2021elephant,Sukesh2024camped}.

\section{Method}
\textbf{Design, hypotheses and rule.} The unit of analysis is the cell: one block (a V1--V3 pair) evaluated on one source. Each block is labelled with its registered hypothesis (H3--H18), so it can be traced to the register. In each block, V1 and V3 share data, hyperparameters, base augmentation and machine, except H16, whose V1 are those of H15, trained on another machine of the same model; V3 adds occlusion augmentation only in training. H3 and H4 predicted that the synthetic gain would carry over to real occlusion; the later transfer hypotheses predict that it does not appear when the occluder (H9, H9b, H10, H14; OE2), the detector (H12, H15, H17, and H8 with unequal exposure; OE3), the domain (H6; OE4), data volume (H7, exploratory; OE4) or the dose (H18; OE2) change. The prediction holds (transfer \emph{not detected}) if fewer than two of the three real sources \emph{pass}, i.e., improve AP@0.5 by at least +2 pp (mean over seeds) with a 95\,\% image-level CI that excludes zero, and the synthetic control does pass; if the control fails, the block is inconclusive. H11 predicted the opposite (a gain on the most occluded CityPersons people), H16a and H16b that the Faster R-CNN transfer would extend to other occluders, and H13 that patches would be more distinguishable from real occlusion than silhouettes, and these than diffusion (OE5). The programme began with H1, H2 and H5, on an attention module (CBAM, variants V2 and V4) and the synthetic gain of silhouettes: H1 and H2 did not hold, H5 did (supplement), and the question was reoriented to transfer. Each hypothesis, its rule and its code were registered internally with a SHA-256 cryptographic fingerprint before training its block and none was reformulated; the fingerprint shows that the files did not change, but the project itself recorded their date (external timestamp and Zenodo deposit, DOI \href{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24148406}{\mbox{10.5281/zenodo.24148406}}, only since 10 November 2026). The programme is adaptive (Fig.~\ref{fig:prog}): the question was reoriented after the first experiment, H9b--H17 were designed after seeing earlier results and H18 answers an external review. Anything marked as post hoc decides no hypothesis.

\textbf{Data.} ExDark~\cite{Loh2019}, with the split of Cui et al.~\cite{Cui2021}, has 2,658 images with people; 1,183 are used for training. Real-occlusion sources: (1) NightOwls~\cite{Neumann2019}, at night and from a vehicle, with its authors' binary occlusion attribute (530 occluded pedestrians in 2,000 validation frames); (2) CityPersons~\cite{Zhang2017}, by day, \emph{heavy} (visibility 20--65\,\%, height $\geq$50 px); (3) a proxy of occlusion between people in ExDark from box overlap. The proxy was adopted after H3 and H4 (13/09/2026), because the planned manual annotation was unreliable ($\kappa$ 0.06--0.60 between passes), and, applied to CityPersons, it agrees poorly with the annotated visibility (Spearman $\rho$ and $\kappa$ = 0.24); from H6 on it was fixed before training, and we repeat the analysis without it (Section~IV). It is a secondary source; it stays in the ``2 of 3'' rule because it was registered that way from H6 on. CityPersons and NightOwls are scored with the full-body box, as in their protocols, and ExDark with the box of its annotations; a synthetically occluded person keeps its full box and pasted person silhouettes are labelled (up to 0.13, 0.5 and 1.75 people per image depending on the dose). All three datasets are used under non-commercial research licences.

\textbf{Augmentation, dose and control.} Occluder families: patches; silhouettes of real objects and people; diffusion (Stable Diffusion 1.5~\cite{Rombach2022ldm}); silhouettes with the real visibility geometry of CityPersons; and depth (Depth Anything V2~\cite{Yang2024depthv2}). The usual dose occludes an image with probability 0.5, and in it 1--3 people with coverage from 0.30 to 0.85 (0.40--0.95 with the real geometry; for diffusion and depth, the occluded image replaces the original with probability 0.5). H18e changes only the silhouette dose: low (probability 0.25, one person, coverage 0.15--0.40) and high (1.0; 1--6 people; 0.50--0.95). The \emph{control} applies the same generator to every person in the test set of the training domain with coverage from 0.70 to 1.00 and unseen occluders; the cross-family matrix uses the other families' controls (parameters in the supplement).

\textbf{Detectors and exposure.} YOLOv11s, YOLOv11m~\cite{Ultralytics2024}, YOLO26s~\cite{Ultralytics2026yolo26}, RT-DETR-l~\cite{Zhao2024rtdetr} and, from torchvision (ResNet-50 FPN), Faster R-CNN~\cite{Ren2017faster} and RetinaNet~\cite{Lin2017focal}, pre-trained on COCO~\cite{Lin2014}, at 640 px on the long side (recipes in the supplement). In H4, H4-Y26, H6 and H8 each variant was selected by validation, with \emph{unequal} exposure (V1 at epochs 1--51, V3 at 31--89); from H9b on, V1 and V3 are trained for 30 fixed epochs (60 in H11) and evaluated with the last weights: these 14 blocks are the primary evidence. Each variant is trained with 3 seeds and H18a with 5 (H9b and H10-s were extended to 5 separately; here their seeds 0--2 are used). In total there were 133 runs and 109 GPU hours (log in the supplement); each 3-seed block costs 0.5--2.6 h on an RTX 4090. RT-DETR-l and YOLO26s only have blocks with unequal exposure (H8, H4-Y26), so the primary evidence covers four detectors. Machines: a laptop with an RTX 5050 (8 GB) and, for H11, H12 and H14--H18, a rented RTX 4090. V3 barely improves on validation between epochs 25 and 30 ($<$0.6 pp), and H11, with 60 epochs, does not transfer either (supplement).

\textbf{Evaluation.} AP@0.5 of \emph{person} by occlusion stratum, with the other strata as \emph{ignore} regions~\cite{Zhang2017}; it is the only metric common to the three sources and the control. CIs by \emph{bootstrap} over images (1,000 replicates)~\cite{Efron1993}, which decides the rule, and hierarchical over seeds and images (2,000), applied to every cell. With 3 seeds there are only 10 distinct seed resamples and the hierarchical CI underestimates the between-seed variance; as a sensitivity we give the Welch $t$ CI from the per-seed AP of V1 and V3 (supplement). The +2 pp threshold is the smallest effect of interest: it equals, for Faster R-CNN, about 17 more occluded people detected per 1,000 at 0.1 false positives per image (post hoc analysis), and exceeds the between-seed SD of V1 (median 1.5 pp). Not detecting transfer does not prove its absence: with a true +2 pp on all three sources the rule detects it in one block out of three (with +3 pp, in 94\,\%). A cell \emph{rules out} +2 pp if the upper limit of its CI is below +2~\cite{Lakens2017tost}. As a global estimate (post hoc analysis) we fit a mixed model by REML to the per-seed differences: a fixed effect per source and random effects for the block and for the seed within the block. Sensitivity: a component for V1 shared across blocks and a block bootstrap; as a one-sided non-superiority test, we compare the upper limit of the 90\,\% CI with +2 pp. Specificity (post hoc) is the pp difference between occluded and unoccluded people, the same scale as the rule and the +2 pp threshold; since the base AP of occluded people is much lower, the supplement also gives it on a relative scale and in miss rate. Secondary metrics (supplement) agree: with MR$^{-2}$~\cite{Dollar2012}, 4 of 51 real cells improve (3 on ExDark); with AP@0.5:0.95, none of 24 in the first eight blocks.

\section{Results}
\textbf{Degradation (OE1).} In the 10 baselines trained on ExDark with equal exposure, AP@0.5 goes from 0.61--0.69 unoccluded to 0.15--0.21 on occluded NightOwls people, from 0.47--0.65 to 0.08--0.15 on CityPersons \emph{heavy} and from 0.78--0.85 to 0.20--0.31 on severe ExDark occlusion.

\textbf{Global estimate (OE2--OE4).} All figures are V3$-$V1 in AP@0.5 (Table~\ref{tab:res}). All 20 controls pass, from +2.6 to +60.9 pp. With the usual dose and equal exposure (12 blocks, 114 per-seed differences), the mixed model estimates +0.54 [$-$0.40; +1.48] on NightOwls, +1.03 [+0.09; +1.97] on CityPersons and $-1.13$ [$-$2.08; $-$0.19] on ExDark (95\,\% CI): with the 90\,\% CI (non-superiority; Fig.~\ref{fig:bosque}), the upper limits are +1.33, +1.82 and $-0.34$, hardly compatible with a mean gain of 2 pp; the CityPersons limit is +1.93 with shared V1 and +1.51 with a block bootstrap, so no specification reaches +2 at the usual dose. Adding the block's gain on its control does not change the estimate (+0.16 pp per SD [$-$0.55; +0.87]; per-source scatter in the supplement). Including the dose blocks (H18e), the 90\,\% upper limits rise to +2.11 on NightOwls and +2.13 on CityPersons, above the threshold. Descriptively, of those 36 cells 5 pass with the hierarchical CI, 9 rule out +2 pp and 22 are inconclusive (with the Welch CI, 3, 11 and 22); over all usual-dose blocks with a control, 5 of 54 pass, against 1.2 expected by chance: gains are few, but more than chance would give.

\textbf{Exposure.} When V1 and V3 are selected at different epochs, the mean effect is $-4.94$ pp; the harm of silhouettes on NightOwls ($-16.8$ in H4) shrinks to $-1.12$ with fixed epochs (H10-s): unequal exposure explains the harm, but equalizing it produces no gain.

\textbf{Detector, domain and data (OE3, OE4).} With equal exposure, the pattern repeats with YOLOv11m (H12); with unequal exposure, RT-DETR-l (H8) and YOLO26s show the same sign, but those blocks do not allow a conclusion. Faster R-CNN with silhouettes (H15) passes the rule on NightOwls (+3.28) and CityPersons (+2.33), but with the between-seed variance both CIs include zero (Welch: [$-$0.14; +6.71] and [$-$0.86; +5.52]): the pass was within seed noise, which anticipates its replication. The 5-seed replication on another machine (H18a) keeps NightOwls (+4.26; Welch [+2.63; +5.89]) but not CityPersons (+1.74 [$-$0.08; +3.69], compatible with the +2.33 of H15): 1 of 3. The tests that bound H15 do not transfer either: with diffusion and depth (H16a, H16b), Faster R-CNN learns the augmentation (+20.35 and +9.63) and no real cell exceeds +0.92, and RetinaNet with silhouettes (H17) gives 0 of 3, with a V3 that worsens on all three real sources. Training in the same domain is not enough either: within CityPersons (H6, H11) the control gains about +24 and \emph{heavy} less than +2.

\textbf{Dose (OE2).} With Faster R-CNN, the gain grows with the dose on the control (+2.9, +14.8 and +25.6 pp with seeds 0--2; the table gives +14.44 with 5), on NightOwls and on CityPersons, but not on ExDark (Fig.~\ref{fig:dosis}; ExDark in Table~\ref{tab:res}). The high dose (H18e-h) passes the rule on two sources; with Welch, only NightOwls excludes zero ([+6.61; +10.56]; CityPersons [$-$0.85; +8.41]). In pp, unoccluded people gain as much or more (+8.87 and +5.34); on a relative scale the gain is larger for occluded people (+60\,\% against +14\,\% on NightOwls, with base AP 0.14 and 0.63), and in miss rate it is smaller (supplement): specificity depends on the scale. Overall ExDark AP drops by 1.98.

\textbf{Occluder (OE2).} Neither diffusion, nor real geometry, nor depth meets the rule; in the cross-family matrix, 13 of 20 cells pass.

\textbf{Post hoc analyses (OE5).} \emph{Without ExDark}, requiring NightOwls and CityPersons, only Faster R-CNN with silhouettes (H15) and its high dose (H18e-h) detect transfer; at the usual dose, only H15, which does not meet the rule again with five seeds. \emph{Specificity} (in pp): subtracting the gain on unoccluded people, of the 6 cells in H3--H17 that pass the rule (image CI), only the two from the ExDark proxy are specific, and none withstands the hierarchical CI. \emph{Visible box:} scoring CityPersons with the visible box, the \emph{heavy} gain exceeds +1.3 in no block of H3--H17 (H15: +0.74): part of what is gained is estimating the full extent of the person. \emph{Realism} (H13, partially confirmed): with DINOv2~\cite{Oquab2024dinov2}, patches are more distinguishable from real occlusion than silhouettes, but silhouettes are not more so than diffusion; each family's occlusion is told apart from real CityPersons occlusion (AUC 0.94--0.99) more easily than an unoccluded person (0.88).

\section{Discussion}
As in Taori et al.~\cite{Taori2020shifts} and Pytel et al.~\cite{Pytel2021tilting}, what is gained against a synthetic perturbation barely carries over to the real one; this does not contradict Kassaw et al.~\cite{Kassaw2026occl} (synthetic occlusion can rank models and still not suffice as a training signal) and, as in Hendrycks et al.~\cite{Hendrycks2021manyfaces} and Mintun et al.~\cite{Mintun2021interaction}, carry-over depends on the augmentation--test pair and on the dose. The high dose improves the external sources for visible and occluded people alike; regularization against domain shift would explain it, as would the extra positives (up to $\times$3.5 pasted, labelled people, as in Copy-Paste~\cite{Ghiasi2021copypaste}), and neither reading is robustness to occlusion; even so, a non-specific gain helps detect occluded people. Nor is the global estimate null on every source: CityPersons shows a small but non-zero gain (+1.03 [+0.09; +1.97]) and the ExDark proxy a harm ($-$1.13 [$-$2.08; $-$0.19]; negative in 9 of 12 blocks and with several occluder families). A tentative reading, which we did not test, is that augmentation moves the model towards pasted occlusion and away from the person-to-person occlusion that this proxy measures. That passing the control is not enough is compatible with a shortcut~\cite{Geirhos2020shortcut}: the artificial occluder remains distinguishable from the real one (H13), although we did not test this confirmatorily. For the 14,722 municipal cameras~\cite{INEI2024renamu}, validating a model on synthetic occlusion alone does not guarantee that it detects occluded people at night.

\textbf{Limitations.} \emph{Scope:} 1,183 training images, fine-tuning from COCO and three doses tested only with Faster R-CNN and silhouettes; at another scale or dose the result may change. \emph{Data:} almost all real occlusion is in datasets other than the training one; CityPersons is daytime, NightOwls is recorded from a vehicle and the ExDark proxy is secondary (III); everything is single-class AP@0.5 at 640 px. \emph{Detectors and data:} RT-DETR-l and YOLO26s only have blocks with unequal exposure; a fixed-epoch RT-DETR-l block was registered and sealed before training (H19, 11 November 2026) but not run for budget reasons; data volume was only explored (H7, no control). \emph{Protocol:} three seeds in most blocks, with which the hierarchical CI is narrow; cells and blocks share baselines (sensitivity in IV), with no multiplicity correction (comparison with chance in IV); unequal exposure in the first blocks; the mixed model is post hoc, and the fingerprints only have an external timestamp (OpenTimestamps) since 10 November 2026. \emph{Future work:} a dose curve with other detectors, at larger scale and without labelling the pasted people; occlusion mined from real images and amodal supervision~\cite{Reddy2022walt}; night-time domain adaptation and part-based architectures~\cite{Wang2020compnet}.

\section{Conclusion}
Real occlusion degrades the baseline in all three sources (OE1). Augmentation improved detection on synthetic occlusion in the 20 blocks with a control. On real occlusion, with the usual dose and equal exposure (12 blocks), the (post hoc) mixed model is hardly compatible with a mean gain of 2 pp on all three sources (OE2--OE4), also with shared V1 or a block bootstrap, though not when the doses are included; the only combination that passed the rule, Faster R-CNN with silhouettes, did not meet the rule again with five seeds. A high dose does improve NightOwls (and CityPersons with the image CI), but by the same pp for unoccluded people: in absolute difference it is not specific to occlusion, although on a relative scale the gain is larger for occluded people. The synthetic occluder remains distinguishable from the real one (OE5). With pixel-level augmentation, 1,183 training images and single-class AP@0.5, passing a synthetic control is not enough to claim robustness to real occlusion: it takes annotated real occlusion, V1 and V3 on the same machine, five seeds, a CI with the between-seed variance and a specificity criterion with its scale stated.
